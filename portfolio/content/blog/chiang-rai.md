Chiang Rai is the quiet north of Thailand: fewer crowds than Chiang Mai, temples
that look like they were drawn rather than built, and a border where three
countries meet in the middle of a river. I spent four days there, from the
Thursday evening of 11 September to Sunday 14 September, and walked most of it.

## Thursday — arriving at night

Landing in the evening left just enough time for the city centre. I started at
the **Clock Tower**, the golden one that lights up and plays music after dark,
then wandered the streets around it before ending at the **night bazaar**. It's
the usual mix — food stalls, souvenirs, live music at the back — but it's a good,
cheap first meal and a gentle way to get a feel for the size of the place. Chiang
Rai's centre is small; you can cross it in twenty minutes.

## Friday — the big loop

The busiest day by far, and the one worth planning around.

![Wat Rong Khun, the White Temple, reflected in its pond under a blue sky](/blog/chiang-rai/cover.jpg)

**Wat Rong Khun**, the White Temple, is the postcard. It deserves the reputation:
entirely white, covered in mirrored glass that catches the sun, and much stranger
up close than the photos suggest — the bridge to the entrance crosses a field of
sculpted hands reaching up out of the ground. Go early, before the tour buses.

**Wat Rong Suea Ten**, the Blue Temple, is its opposite: deep indigo walls, gold
detail, and a huge white Buddha inside. It's smaller and takes fifteen minutes,
but the contrast right after the White Temple is the point.

**Baan Dam**, the Black House museum, completes the set. It's not a temple at all
— it's the life's work of an artist, a collection of dark wooden buildings filled
with animal bones, hides and furniture made of horns. Unsettling, and the most
memorable of the three.

From there the day headed north: a **long-neck Karen village**, the **Choui Fong
tea plantation** with its rows of green hills you can walk into, and finally the
**Golden Triangle**, where Thailand, Laos and Myanmar meet across the Mekong. The
**Opium Museum** next to it explains why that particular bend of the river was
worth so much to so many people. It's a heavy subject and the museum handles it
better than the viewpoint selfie spots outside.

## Saturday — the hike to Lalitta

Slower day, better day. I walked up towards **Wat Huay Pla Kang** — the enormous
white Guan Yin statue you can see from most of the city — stopping at the smaller
temples along the path on the way up. **Lalitta Café** sits right next to it:
little huts, ponds, bridges, extremely staged and honestly a good place to sit
down after the climb.

The evening was the **Saturday night market** on Thanalai Road, which is a
different animal from the nightly bazaar — longer, louder, more locals, better
food. Both are worth doing; the Saturday one if you have to pick.

## Sunday — the forest, and a wrong turn

The last morning was backpacking through the forest outside town to reach a
temple, which went well until the trail stopped being a trail and I walked
straight into **a military base** without realising it. It ended with a polite
escort back out and no drama, but: check the map before following a path that
looks like a shortcut.

The afternoon was the **Chiang Rai Contemporary Art Museum**, a calm place to
finish, and then the walk to the airport — which is doable, flat, and gives you a
last hour of the countryside before the flight home.

## What I'd keep

Three things stayed with me: the Black House, the tea plantation, and the walk up
to Lalitta. The White Temple is the reason people come, but the days with the
least planning in them were the ones I remember best.
