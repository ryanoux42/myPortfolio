Five days in Hanoi in March, which is the good window: warm enough, grey enough
that you're not walking around in 35 degrees, and the city is at its best in that
soft light. This is a place to do on foot, with frequent coffee stops.

## Day 1 — the Old Quarter

The **Old Quarter** is the reason to come. Thirty-six streets, each historically
dedicated to one trade, all of them now full of scooters, plastic stools and
food. There's no efficient way to see it; you just walk.

**Cà phê Giảng** is the mandatory stop — the café that invented **egg coffee**,
served in a tiny cup of whipped yolk and condensed milk over strong Vietnamese
coffee. It sounds wrong and it is excellent. **Đồng Xuân Market**, the largest
covered market in the city, is three floors of everything, and best at ground
level for the food.

Temples next: **Bạch Mã**, the oldest in the Old Quarter, and **Ngọc Sơn**, on its
own island in **Hoàn Kiếm Lake**, reached by a red wooden bridge. The lake is the
centre of the city and worth a full lap. Further out, the **Temple of Literature**
— Vietnam's first university, built in 1070, and the calmest place in Hanoi.

Then **Hanoi train street**, the residential alley so narrow that people pull in
their chairs when the train scrapes past. Access comes and goes depending on the
authorities; the cafés along it will tell you the timetable.

The evening was the **Thăng Long Water Puppet Theatre**, puppets performed waist
deep in a pool of water by puppeteers hidden behind a screen — a thousand-year-old
tradition from the Red River delta, and an hour well spent.

## Day 2 — the French Quarter

The other Hanoi: wide boulevards, plane trees and colonial facades. **St Joseph's
Cathedral**, blackened neo-gothic that looks transplanted from France, and the
**Opera House**, modelled on the Palais Garnier.

Then the political quarter: the **Presidential Palace** in its mustard yellow,
**Hồ Chí Minh's stilt house** in the gardens behind it — he refused to live in the
palace, so he had a wooden house built beside it — and the **Hồ Chí Minh
Mausoleum**. The mausoleum has short morning hours, a strict dress code and a long
queue; check that it's open before planning the day around it.

## Day 3 — incense and the citadel

A morning at the **incense village** of Quảng Phú Cầu, an hour south of the city,
where bundles of red incense sticks are fanned out to dry in courtyards. It is as
photogenic as it looks, and it is a working village — ask before pointing a camera
at people.

The afternoon was the **Thăng Long Imperial Citadel**, the seat of Vietnamese power
for almost eight centuries and a UNESCO site, with a military command bunker from
the American war preserved underneath it.

## Day 4 — Ninh Binh

The day trip everyone tells you to take, correctly. **Ninh Bình** — "Ha Long Bay
on land" — is two hours south: limestone karsts rising straight out of rice
paddies, with a rowing boat taking you through caves at water level at Tam Cốc or
Tràng An, and a viewpoint at Hang Múa reached by about 500 steps. Long day, early
start, the best photographs of the trip.

## Day 5 — the last two stops

**Hỏa Lò Prison**, the "Hanoi Hilton", used by the French for Vietnamese political
prisoners and later for American pilots. The museum is heavily one-sided and still
very much worth seeing. Then **Trấn Quốc Pagoda**, the oldest Buddhist temple in
Hanoi, on a small peninsula in West Lake — a red stupa tower over the water, and a
quiet place to end.

## Practical notes

Crossing the street is the skill to learn: walk slowly, predictably, and don't
stop — the scooters will go around you. Grab is cheap for the further sights.
And leave a full day for Ninh Bình rather than squeezing it into a half.
