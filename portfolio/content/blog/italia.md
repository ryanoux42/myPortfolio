Two cities, a week, and the middle of August, which is the part I'd think
harder about next time. Italy in August is hot and everyone else is also on
holiday, so the queues are at their longest exactly when standing in them is
worst.

## Milan

Milan gets written off as the business city, which isn't fair to it. The
**Duomo** alone justifies the stop: a cathedral that took roughly six centuries
to finish, with a roof you can walk on. Next door, the **Galleria Vittorio
Emanuele II** is a shopping arcade from the 1870s under an iron-and-glass dome,
and it connects to **La Scala**, which is smaller inside than its reputation
suggests.

![The Duomo's white marble facade and spires seen across Piazza del Duomo](/blog/italia/duomo-facade.jpg)

![The arched entrance to the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, crowded with people on the square outside](/blog/italia/galleria-vittorio-emanuele.jpg)

The statue of **Leonardo da Vinci** stands in the square right outside La
Scala, surrounded by his students, which felt like the right place to find
him.

![The Leonardo da Vinci monument in Piazza della Scala, framed by trees](/blog/italia/leonardo-da-vinci-monument.jpg)

![The red and gold tiered boxes inside La Scala, seen looking up from the stalls](/blog/italia/la-scala-interior.jpg)

The **Castello Sforzesco** took up most of a morning. It's a fortress with
about a dozen separate museums inside it, so you either pick or you lose the
day. We also did the **Museo del Novecento** for twentieth-century Italian art
and the **Leonardo da Vinci science museum**, which is enormous and worth more
time than we gave it. Milan's smaller galleries are worth a detour too: this
vase of flowers stopped me for longer than most other things I photographed
that day.

![A framed old master still life painting of a vase overflowing with flowers, on a dark blue gallery wall](/blog/italia/flower-still-life-painting.jpg)

Book ahead for anything with a fixed slot. The Last Supper at Santa Maria delle
Grazie is the notorious one: tickets go weeks in advance and there's no turning
up on the day.

Evenings were the **Navigli**, the canal district, which is where the city
stops being about opening hours, and where I finally sat down for a proper
tiramisu.

![The Navigli canal at sunset, lined with restaurants and people on both banks](/blog/italia/navigli-canal-sunset.jpg)

![A slice of tiramisu on a plate at a table by the canal](/blog/italia/milan-tiramisu.jpg)

## The train south

We took the train between the two cities rather than flying. Four hours, city
centre to city centre, watching the country change through the window. It was
genuinely one of the better parts of the week, which I did not expect to be
writing about a train.

## Ancient Rome

Rome's problem is that there's too much of it and it's all within walking
distance of itself.

![The Colosseum from across the road under a clear sky, with a queue of visitors along the front](/blog/italia/cover.jpg)

The **Colosseum** and the **Roman Forum** are one long morning if you do them
properly, and the walk up Via dei Fori Imperiali afterwards takes you to the
Capitoline and its museums, which hold the original bronze she-wolf and a
courtyard of giant marble body parts from a statue of Constantine.

![The Roman Forum's columns and ruins seen from above, with the white Vittoriano monument in the background](/blog/italia/roman-forum-from-palatine.jpg)

Right beside the Capitoline is the **Vittoriano**, the enormous white monument
to Vittorio Emanuele II, with an eternal flame and two soldiers standing guard
over the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at its base.

![Two soldiers standing guard at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at the base of the Vittoriano monument](/blog/italia/vittoriano-monument.jpg)

The **Pantheon** is a short walk from there and still has its original dome,
nearly two thousand years old, with the dedication to Marcus Agrippa carved
above the columns exactly as it always was.

![The Pantheon's columned facade and dome seen from the fountain and obelisk in Piazza della Rotonda](/blog/italia/pantheon.jpg)

## The rest of it

The **Trevi Fountain** is worth timing for early evening, once the heat drops
and the light turns the stone gold.

![The Trevi Fountain lit up gold at sunset, with statues rising above the pool](/blog/italia/trevi-fountain-at-sunset.jpg)

The **Vatican** needs booking in advance too: St Peter's Basilica, then the
Sistine Chapel at the end of a very long museum route that doesn't let you
double back.

![St Peter's Basilica and its obelisk seen across St Peter's Square under a clear blue sky](/blog/italia/st-peters-basilica.jpg)

Away from the headline sights, the best few hours were on the **Janiculum**
hill: the view over the whole city, the Acqua Paola fountain, and a quiet park
at the top. Below it, **Trastevere** is the part of Rome I'd go back to first,
narrow streets, no monuments to tick off, and the right place to sit outside
with a spritz. **Campo de' Fiori** and the **Villa Borghese** gardens filled
the last day.

## Seventeen hours home

The flight back was cancelled by a strike. Instead of ninety minutes to France
we got a rerouting through Frankfurt and seventeen hours door to door, which
was funnier than it should have been by about hour twelve. Worth knowing that
Italian transport strikes are common enough in August to be a planning
assumption rather than bad luck.
