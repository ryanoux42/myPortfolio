I landed on the Thursday and a friend was waiting at the airport, which is the
best possible way to arrive anywhere. We hadn't seen each other in a year,
since I'd left for a year-long exchange in Thailand around the same time he'd
come to Laos for a humanitarian mission, so this was as much a reunion as a
trip.

## The temples

Luang Prabang sits on a narrow peninsula where the Nam Khan runs into the
Mekong, and the old town packed into it has more temples than seems reasonable
for its size. Two stood out.

**Wat Sensoukharam** is the one you can't walk past: a wall of red and gold
right on the main road through town, built in the early 1700s and repainted
often enough that it still looks new.

![Wat Sensoukharam's red and gold facade on the main road, with two golden lion statues guarding the steps](/blog/luang-prabang/wat-sensoukharam.jpg)

**Wat Xieng Thong** is the one people actually come for. It's at the very tip of
the peninsula, dates from the 1560s, and the roofs are the whole point: they
sweep down almost to the ground in layers, which is a style you see here and
basically nowhere else. The mosaic tree of life on the back wall is worth
walking around for.

![The view over Luang Prabang from the hill in the middle of town, with the Nam Khan river, a bamboo footbridge and mountains behind](/blog/luang-prabang/cover.jpg)

## The climb

The hill in the middle of town, Phousi, is a few hundred steps and gets you the
photo above: the whole peninsula, the river, one improbably thin footbridge,
and mountains stacked up behind. It's the standard thing to do at sunset, which
means it's busy at sunset. Going up at another time costs you nothing.

![A brick staircase lined with white posts winding up through the trees on Phousi hill](/blog/luang-prabang/phousi-hill-stairs.jpg)

## A day with the mission

I spent a day tagging along on my friend's mission instead of sightseeing.
He was teaching, so I sat in on his class and helped out where I could. One of
the boards he'd made for the kids was a colours-and-emotions sheet, half in
French and half in English, which says a lot about what the mission actually
looks like day to day.

![A hand-drawn "Couleurs Emotions" flashcard in French and English, used to teach the kids colours and feelings](/blog/luang-prabang/french-class-flashcard.jpg)

## The night market

After dark the main street shuts to traffic and the night market unrolls down
the middle of it: textiles, lamps, food, and shopfronts strung with lanterns
and Laos flags. It's the centre of the evening here rather than a side
attraction, partly because Luang Prabang is small and quiet and doesn't offer
a great deal else once the temples close.

![Lanterns lit up red in a tree above a night market bar, with Laos flags and paper stars, people at tables underneath](/blog/luang-prabang/night-market.jpg)

## Kuang Si Falls

We took a day out of town to **Kuang Si Falls**, a stack of turquoise pools
running down through the jungle outside Luang Prabang, fed by mineral-rich
water that gives it that colour. On the way in there's a rescue centre for
Asiatic black bears saved from poachers, which we walked through on the way
to the falls, a strange detour but a good one.

![The tiered turquoise pools of Kuang Si Falls cascading down through the jungle](/blog/luang-prabang/kuang-si-falls.jpg)

## Small on purpose

Four days is more than enough for a town this size, and that turned out to be
the appeal rather than a problem. The whole place runs slowly and there's no
real pressure to see everything, because there isn't that much to see. Getting
to see a year of my friend's life there up close mattered more than the
temples did. I'd rather have that than another capital.
